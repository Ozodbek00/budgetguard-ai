# Detection methodology

How BudgetGuard AI decides that a payment, a supplier, or a whole ledger is
worth an auditor's time — and, just as importantly, where it can be wrong.

Every method here is one a human can re-derive with a spreadsheet. That is the
point of the product. Black-box anomaly detection fails in audit not because it
is inaccurate but because a finding that cannot be explained cannot be acted on:
an auditor cannot open an investigation, and certainly cannot support a
sanction, on the grounds that a model scored something 0.87.

---

## The problem

Public procurement oversight today runs on two unsatisfying options.

**Manual audit** is trusted but does not scale. A reviewer sampling 200 of
50,000 payments finds what is in the sample.

**Machine-learning anomaly detection** scales but is not trusted. It produces a
ranked list with no reviewable reasoning, so the output is either taken on faith
or ignored. In an accountability context, where a finding may end up in a
tribunal, "the model flagged it" is not a finding.

BudgetGuard AI takes a third route: **classical statistical forensics, fully
explained.** The techniques are decades old and court-tested. The contribution
is applying them together, automatically, with the arithmetic behind every flag
exposed to the person who has to act on it.

---

## Method 1 — Benford's Law (first-digit conformity)

### The theory

In data that spans several orders of magnitude and arises from multiplicative
processes, leading digits are not uniformly distributed. The probability that a
value begins with digit *d* is:

```
P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d)
```

| Digit | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expected | 30.1% | 17.6% | 12.5% | 9.7% | 7.9% | 6.7% | 5.8% | 5.1% | 4.6% |

The underlying reason is scale invariance. If a quantity is equally likely to be
measured in any unit — som, thousands of som, dollars — its logarithm is
approximately uniform, and a uniform mantissa in log space produces exactly this
digit law. Procurement spending qualifies: contract values are generated
multiplicatively (quantity × unit price × duration) and span from stationery
orders to highway contracts.

### Why it catches fraud

People inventing numbers do not reproduce that curve. Two signatures dominate in
procurement:

- **Round-number invoicing.** Fabricated amounts cluster on human-chosen
  figures — 25,000,000; 50,000,000 — over-representing digits 2, 3, 5 and 7.
- **Threshold evasion.** Where a 50,000,000 ceiling triggers open tender,
  contracts pile up just underneath it, producing a glut of amounts starting
  with 4. This is the single most common procurement manipulation, and it is
  extremely visible in a first-digit histogram.

Benford's Law does not identify a guilty transaction. It says *this population
was not generated by the process that generates honest spending*. That is a cue
to pull the ledger, not a conclusion.

### How conformity is judged

The primary test is **Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)** — the average absolute gap
between observed and expected proportion across the nine digits — assessed
against Nigrini's published bands for first-digit tests:

| MAD | Verdict |
|---|---|
| < 0.006 | Close conformity |
| 0.006 – 0.012 | Acceptable conformity |
| 0.012 – 0.015 | Marginally acceptable |
| > 0.015 | **Non-conformity** |

**Chi-square is computed and reported, but is deliberately not the verdict.**
Chi-square scales with sample size: on 50,000 rows it rejects conformity for
deviations far too small to mean anything, so using it as a decision rule would
flag almost every large real dataset. It appears in the UI as supporting
information only.

### The sample-size correction

Nigrini's bands assume a large population. Applying them to a single vendor's
invoices does not work, and this was found by testing the detector against
*clean* synthetic data rather than only against planted fraud.

If a sample of size *n* follows Benford's Law perfectly, each observed
proportion still wobbles around its expectation with standard error
√(p(1−p)/n). Averaging the expected absolute deviation across the nine digits
gives the MAD that chance alone produces:

```
E[MAD | conforming] ≈ 0.2348 / √n
```

At n = 100 that is **0.024 — already above the 0.015 non-conformity band.** So a
naive per-vendor Benford test flags essentially every vendor, which is exactly
what the first implementation did: a clean 1,200-row ledger produced dozens of
Critical findings against innocent suppliers.

BudgetGuard therefore requires a deviation to clear **both** the fixed band and
a multiple of the sampling-noise floor:

```
threshold(n) = max(0.015, NoiseFloorMultiple × 0.2348/√n)     default multiple = 2.0
```

On a large dataset the fixed band governs. On an 80-invoice vendor the floor
governs, and only a genuinely manufactured distribution clears it. Every
explanation states which threshold was applied and why.

A hard minimum of 50 usable amounts applies regardless; below that no verdict is
issued at all.

Zero and negative amounts carry no first-digit information and are excluded —
counted and reported, never silently dropped.

## Method 2 — Peer-relative amount outliers (z-score)

### Why grouped, not global

A 900,000,000 som road contract is not suspicious. A 900,000,000 som stationery
order is. Comparing every payment against one dataset-wide mean would flag all
large-ticket categories and miss the anomalies inside small ones.

Amounts are therefore only ever compared against their **peer group** — the same
vendor, the same category, or the same department. That is the comparison an
auditor makes by hand. Each grouping is evaluated independently, so the report
can say *which* peer group a payment is abnormal within.

### The comparison scale — why logarithms

This is the second correction that came out of testing against clean data.

Procurement amounts are heavily right-skewed, close to log-normal. A z-score
assumes a roughly symmetric distribution. Applied to raw currency amounts, the
upper tail of *any* realistic spend distribution sits several standard
deviations above a mean that the tail itself drags upward. Measured on a clean
1,200-row synthetic ledger with no planted anomalies, raw-amount z-scoring
produced **55 High/Critical false positives**. On the same data, comparing
log₁₀(amount) produced **none**, while still catching genuine
order-of-magnitude outliers.

So by default a flag means *"unusual by a factor"*, not *"unusual by an absolute
sum"* — which is the meaningful notion of distance for money. The raw scale
remains available via configuration. Explanations quote the geometric mean in
currency and express dispersion as a multiplicative factor, so the numbers still
read naturally.

### The statistic

Default is the classic z-score with a threshold of **3.0 standard deviations**.
Under a normal distribution roughly 0.27% of observations lie beyond 3σ, so on
10,000 rows that is about 27 flags from chance alone — a reviewable number.
Procurement amounts are not normal even in log space, so this is a screening
heuristic calibrated for a workable queue length, not a probability claim.

A **modified z-score** (Iglewicz–Hoaglin) is also implemented and selectable:

```
M = 0.6745 × (x − median) / MAD          threshold 3.5
```

It exists because the classic z-score has a masking problem: a large outlier
inflates the very standard deviation used to judge it, and a *pair* of outliers
can hide each other completely. The test suite demonstrates this concretely — a
group of eight payments of 100 and two of 1,000 yields z ≈ 1.9 for the large
values, under the threshold, while the modified statistic flags both. The
median-based version is resistant because the values being tested cannot move
the median.

Guard rails: a peer group needs at least 8 members before any flag is possible,
and a group with zero dispersion produces nothing.

The test is **two-tailed**. Unusually *small* payments matter too — contract
splitting to stay under approval ceilings shows up as a cluster of suspiciously
small awards.

## Method 3 — Vendor concentration

### What it looks for

Procurement fraud rarely shows up in one payment; it shows up as a pattern of
awards. A vendor repeatedly winning an outsized share of one category's budget
is the classic signature of bid rigging, a tender specification written around
one supplier, or an undisclosed relationship between a supplier and the
officials awarding the work.

### Two spend thresholds, deliberately

- **Absolute:** more than **30%** of scope spend.
- **Relative:** more than **3× the even-split expectation**, where the
  expectation is 1/N for N competing vendors.

Either firing raises a flag. The relative test matters because 30% means very
different things among 3 suppliers and among 50: in a category with 40 vendors,
15% is already extraordinary, and a fixed percentage threshold would miss it.

### The contract-count requirement

This was the third correction found by testing against clean data, and it is the
most important one.

A single enormous payment gives its vendor a large share of scope spend *by
definition*. So every genuine amount outlier also branded its vendor as
market-dominant — the same anomaly counted twice, under a label that did not
describe it. In demo testing, five innocent suppliers appeared as Critical
concentration findings purely because each happened to hold one planted outlier
payment.

Winning a lot of money is not the same as winning a lot of work. Under an even
award process a vendor's contract count in a scope follows **Binomial(T, 1/N)**,
so a flag now additionally requires a statistically significant excess of
contracts:

```
expected  = T/N
σ         = √(T × 1/N × (1 − 1/N))
z_count   = (observed − expected) / σ        must exceed 2.0
```

plus an absolute floor of 3 contracts. This cleanly separates **market capture**
(many wins — this detector's job) from **an amount outlier** (one big win — the
z-score detector's job). After the change, the demo dataset's false-positive
vendors disappeared and both deliberately planted vendors ranked top.

### Scope-level concentration (HHI)

Independently of any single vendor, each category and department gets a
**Herfindahl–Hirschman Index** — the sum of squared percentage market shares,
from near 0 (perfect competition) to 10,000 (monopoly):

```
HHI = Σ (share_i × 100)²
```

Above **2,500** competition authorities (US DOJ/FTC Horizontal Merger
Guidelines) class a market as highly concentrated. A category scoring badly here
is worth reviewing as a *procurement process* even when no individual vendor
crosses a threshold. An even split among N vendors scores 10,000/N, which the UI
shows alongside the actual figure as a reference point.

## Combining the signals

Each detector emits signals scored on a common 0–1 scale, where sitting exactly
on a threshold scores 0.5 and roughly twice the threshold saturates at 1.0.

A subject's risk score is:

```
risk = min(1, max over detectors(score × weight) + bonus × (distinct detectors − 1))
```

**Scores are not summed.** A transaction flagged as an outlier against its
vendor, its category *and* its department is one anomaly seen three ways, not
three anomalies. Summing would let repetition of a single method outrank
genuinely corroborated evidence.

**Corroboration earns a bonus** (default 0.15 per additional detector). The three
methods read different properties of the data — the digits of an amount, its
distance from peer amounts, and the distribution of awards across suppliers.
They can each fire on innocent data. When two land on the same vendor, that
agreement is real evidence. The bonus is additive and capped, so it can sharpen
a ranking but never manufacture a finding from signals that never fired.

Severity bands: **Critical ≥ 0.75, High ≥ 0.50, Medium ≥ 0.25**, all
configurable.

## Known limitations and false-positive risks

Stated plainly, because a forensic tool that oversells itself is worse than none.

**Benford**
- Requires values spanning multiple orders of magnitude. Data with a natural
  floor or ceiling (per-diem rates, fixed unit-price schedules, capped grants)
  is not Benford-distributed even when entirely honest.
- Fails on small populations by construction; the noise floor mitigates but does
  not eliminate this. Per-vendor verdicts on suppliers with 50–100 invoices
  should be treated as weak evidence.
- A conforming distribution is **not** evidence of honesty. A sophisticated
  actor can fabricate Benford-conforming amounts.

**Outliers**
- Large legitimate contracts exist. Emergency procurement, one-off capital
  projects and framework call-offs will surface here and are not wrongdoing.
- Miscoded categories create fake peer groups. A stationery order booked under
  Construction will look anomalous in both directions.
- The classic z-score is subject to masking; use the modified statistic where
  clustered manipulation is suspected.

**Concentration**
- Legitimate concentration is common. Some categories genuinely have one
  national supplier; framework agreements deliberately consolidate spend;
  a small region may have few qualified contractors.
- Vendor identity is matched on name. Deliberate splitting across related legal
  entities defeats it — the tool cannot see beneficial ownership. This is the
  single largest evasion route and would be the first thing to address with a
  company registry integration.
- Time is not yet a dimension. A vendor capturing a category in one quarter and
  not others is currently averaged out across the whole dataset.

**General**
- The detectors assume the input is complete. Selective omission upstream
  defeats all three.
- Thresholds are screening defaults, not legal standards. They should be tuned
  per jurisdiction against known-clean historical data.
- **Findings are leads for human review. Nothing here is a determination of
  wrongdoing**, and the product states this on every screen.

## Validation approach

Correctness is the product, so the detectors are tested against ground truth
rather than only for absence of crashes.

The synthetic data generator plants four specific manipulations — threshold
evasion, a round-number invoicing vendor, a supplier capturing one category, and
several extreme payments — and **records exactly what it planted**. The test
suite then runs the full pipeline and asserts that each planted anomaly is
found, that clean rows are not flagged, and that the actionable queue stays a
small fraction of the dataset.

That last assertion is what caught all three statistical defects described
above. Testing only that planted fraud is detected would have passed a detector
that flags everything.

Current behaviour on the 1,710-row demo dataset:

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Findings total | 10 |
| Critical | 2 (both deliberately planted vendors) |
| Corroborated by 2+ methods | 1 |
| Planted anomalies detected | 4 of 4 |
| Actionable findings as share of ledger | 0.6% |

The clean-ledger regression test asserts **zero** High or Critical findings on
1,200 rows of honest synthetic spending.
